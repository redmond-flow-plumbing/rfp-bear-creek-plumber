Country Plumbing, City Standards

The fundamental difference on a Bear Creek lot is distance. In the city, water travels fifty feet from the main to your house. Here, it might travel five hundred — from a well head at the back of the property, or along a long private service line from the road. Every foot of pipe is friction, which means pressure loss, which means your shower suffers if the system wasn't designed for the run. We see undersized lines, too many elbows, and pressure tanks set wrong for the distance involved. Then there's the mix: some Bear Creek pockets are on city water, some on private wells, some on shared well agreements with neighbors — and the plumbing strategy differs for each. Our first step on any Bear Creek call is understanding your water source and your layout, because the right fix for a city-water home is often the wrong fix for a well system.

Wells, Pressure Tanks & What We Handle

Let's be clear about boundaries: the well itself — the drilled hole, the submersible pump down in it — belongs to a well specialist. Everything after the well head is ours. The pressure tank is the heart of your system: it stores pressurized water so the pump doesn't cycle every time you wash your hands. When a tank's internal bladder fails, the pump short-cycles — clicking on and off rapidly — which burns out pumps prematurely and sends your electric bill climbing. Pressure switches get out of adjustment, gauges lie, and sediment from the well clogs whole-house filters and fixture aerators. We diagnose the full chain: pressure readings at multiple points, tank drawdown tests, switch calibration, and filter strategy. If your pressure surges and sags, or the pump runs constantly, don't just live with it — the pump is telling you something, and listening early is always cheaper than replacing a burned-out pump later.

Long Runs and Freeze Risk

Pacific Northwest winters are mild until they're not, and Bear Creek's exposed runs are the first casualties of a hard freeze. Water lines to barns, workshops, guest cottages, and irrigation systems often run above the frost line or through unheated spaces — exactly where a week of 20-degree nights splits them. We winterize seasonal lines every fall: shut off, drain, and blow out anything that doesn't need to survive winter. For year-round outbuildings, we specify proper insulation, heat tape on vulnerable sections (with thermostats, not the always-on kind that wastes electricity), and frost-free hose bibbs. Inside the main house, pay attention to pipes in exterior walls and crawlspaces — the classic burst happens in the wall behind the kitchen sink on the north side. A little prevention in October beats a lot of drywall repair in January. Ask us about a freeze-protection walkthrough; it's the highest-value hour we sell all winter.

Septic-Adjacent Smarts

Many Bear Creek properties run septic systems, and while we don't pump tanks (that's a septic specialist's job), everything you put down your drains affects that system — which makes us your first line of defense. The rules are simple but non-negotiable: no garbage disposal abuse (better yet, minimal disposal use — compost instead), no "flushable" wipes (they're not), no grease down the kitchen drain, no harsh chemical drain cleaners that kill the beneficial bacteria your septic depends on. Spread laundry loads across the week instead of doing six loads on Saturday; hydraulic overloading is a real cause of drainfield failure. And know where your drainfield is — then keep vehicles, heavy equipment, and deep-rooted plantings off it. A healthy septic system is invisible. The fastest way to meet your septic pumper on an emergency basis is to treat your drains like a city sewer. When Bear Creek homeowners follow these habits, their systems last decades instead of years.
